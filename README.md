# Statecraft in Prose: Federal Purpose and the Public Vernacular

Every public contract runs through a paper conversation in which institutions try to bind future action with words. Across governments and governance systems, procurement speech is the nexus of stated purposes and real action. After ten years around government contracting ("GovCon"), I have witnessed how institutional dialect can obscure the substance of a request or promise--training personnel slowly, but surely, to communicate ineffectively.

This is a small, independent corpus linguistics project examining how institutional speech shape action. In plain terms, that means gathering real samples of writing, juxtaposing them to see how people actually use words in practice, and comparing how federal purpose is stated in official prose versus how it translates into the public vernacular.

You don't need a linguistics degree or a Washington badge to read these notes:

## How We Look at the Text

Instead of guessing at what a writer meant, we line up turns of a public record—the stated public purpose, what the government asked, what the company wrote, and how the reviewer graded it—and check four concrete habits:

1. **Who is doing the work? (Syntactic Agency & Voice)**
   In normal speech, we name who is responsible ("Our engineer checks the system daily"). Institutional writing hides the actor behind passive phrasing ("System checks will be performed"), smothered verbs/nominalizations ("escalation"), or inanimate frameworks.
2. **How firm is the promise? (Modality & Modal Drift)**
   Saying "We will deliver" is different from saying "We aim to support." Checking verb certainty—and how "shall," "will," "must," and "may" drift from their strict FAR Part 2 definitions—shows where commitments are solid and where wiggle room is built in.
3. **Are they answering or just echoing? (Lexical Overlap, Density & Specificity)**
   A reply can repeat the government's exact words or stack abstract nouns ("enterprise-wide mission-critical acquisition support services") without adding real numbers, tools, or steps. Comparing word overlap against concrete details shows whether a passage explains how the work gets done.
4. **How do reviewers read it? (Pragmatic Uptake)**
   Looking at the exact phrases reviewers quote when giving a "Strength" or a "Weakness" shows what kind of writing helps a human reader and what kind causes confusion.

## Paired Data Sources

To measure how institutional dialect reshapes meaning across a contract's lifecycle, this corpus lines up matching pairs from open records:
- **Solicitations vs. Plain-Language Summaries:** Formal RFPs and Performance Work Statements (https://SAM.gov) paired against agency press releases, outreach flyers, or congressional justifications for the same program.
- **Regulatory Clauses vs. Plain Writing Guidance:** Standard FAR Part 52 clauses compared against Federal Plain Language Guidelines (https://plainlanguage.gov) and the Plain Writing Act of 2010 (which exempts regulations, creating the institutional split studied here).
- **Award & Protest Records vs. Public Reporting:** GAO bid protest decisions (https://gao.gov) and https://USAspending.gov award summaries—where administrative judges and reporters have to translate procurement jargon back into plain narrative facts.

## Rules for Using Public Records

Everything here comes from records already open to the public by law (GAO bid protest decisions, https://SAM.gov solicitations, and https://USAspending.gov).

1. Every quote links back to its official `.gov` docket URL.
2. Quotes stay exact—no edited punctuation or out-of-context clipping.
3. Notes describe how the text functions, not legal validity or personal motives—recognizing that procurement prose is shaped by real statutory rules, page limits, and protest pressures.
